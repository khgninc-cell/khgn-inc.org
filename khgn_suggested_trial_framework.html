The KHGN Suggested Trial Framework

A Framework for Genealogical Proof, Built on the Structure of a Trial

What This Is — and What It Isn't

This framework borrows the shape of a courtroom trial to organize how a genealogical proof argument gets built. It is not a description of how real trials work, and it is not the Genealogical Proof Standard published by the Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG). It's something KHGN built on its own terms, drawing what's useful from both, and departing from both wherever the departure serves the actual work of genealogical research better.

Two departures are worth naming plainly, up front, rather than leaving you to discover them later:

  • We borrow the phrase "beyond a reasonable doubt" for its feel, not its legal weight. That phrase is the actual standard of proof in criminal law. Genealogists are not legally bound by it, and the real Genealogical Proof Standard doesn't use that language. We use it here because it captures the right level of confidence a strong conclusion should carry — not because a family history claim is being held to a courtroom's legal standard.
  • A real courtroom verdict is final. Ours deliberately isn't. Real trials have finality built in — double jeopardy, appeals with real limits. Genealogical proof doesn't work that way, and shouldn't pretend to. A record can surface next year that changes everything you thought you knew. This framework builds that openness into the verdict itself, rather than treating "Proven" as a locked door.
This is a self-directed practice framework, not coursework. Using it does not constitute formal education, training credit, a teaching credential, or any accredited instruction. It isn't affiliated with, endorsed by, or equivalent to BCG's certification process. It's simply a way of organizing your own thinking when you sit down to research — nothing more formal than that.

This is meant to build the habit of honest, rigorous proof argument — not to teach source citation formatting, paleography, or jurisdiction-specific record law, and not to stand in for BCG's actual published standard. For the full professional discipline this framework is inspired by, see the Board for Certification of Genealogists.

Part One: Opening Statements — Both Sides, Before You Choose

A real opening statement only argues one side. This framework asks for both, before you commit to either.

Before you decide what you believe, lay out the case for your leading candidate and the case against them — including the strongest version of the argument for whoever else could be the right person. This is deliberate, and it's the most important departure this framework makes from how most people naturally research: most people pick a candidate first, based on a name match or a family story, and only encounter the opposing case later, if at all — usually after they're already attached to being right.

By forcing both charges onto the table before any commitment is made, this framework tries to catch confirmation bias at the earliest possible point, rather than treating it as something to correct for later. If you can't yet state a real, honest case for the alternative candidate, you're not ready to have an opening statement — you're ready to go find one.

What belongs in this stage:

  • The specific research question you're investigating
  • The leading candidate, and the honest case for them
  • The strongest alternative candidate (or explanation), and the honest case for them
  • No conclusion yet — just two theses, stated fairly

Part Two: Trial Testimony, Rebuttal, and Evidence — One Continuous Body

Real trials often separate direct examination, cross-examination, and rebuttal into distinct phases. This framework merges them into one continuous body, because that's a truer reflection of how genealogical research actually unfolds. You rarely gather all your supporting evidence first and only then encounter the complications. In real research, a supporting record, a complicating record, and another supporting record tend to arrive in whatever order the archive happens to hand them to you.

This stage is where you present evidence as you find it, and you're expected to let it talk back to you as you go:

  • Testimony: each piece of evidence, presented plainly — what it says, where it came from
  • Rebuttal: for each piece, immediately ask what could be wrong with it. A second person of the same name? A transcription error? A record that only proves something adjacent to what you need it to prove?
  • Evidence weight: not all evidence is equal. A pension file naming specific children carries more weight than a bare tax list with only a name and acreage. Say so, honestly, as you go — don't save that judgment for later.

The goal of this stage isn't to build the strongest-sounding case. It's to build the most honestly-tested one, one exchange at a time.

Part Three: Closing Arguments — Both Sides, One Final Time

A real closing argument only restates your own side. This framework asks you to restate both sides' strongest points, in your own words, one more time before rendering any verdict.

This is a genuinely harder exercise than it sounds. If you can only summarize your own conclusion and can't fairly restate the best version of the opposing case, that's a sign you never really engaged with it in the first place — you just noted its existence and moved on. A closing argument that can't do justice to both sides isn't a closing argument. It's a highlight reel.

What belongs in this stage:

  • A fair, complete restatement of the case for your conclusion
  • An equally fair, complete restatement of the strongest case against it — not a straw man, the real thing
  • Only after both are stated: your own assessment of which case actually held up better, and why

Part Four: The Verdict — Confident, But Never Closed

Render your verdict here: the belief that your conclusion is true beyond a reasonable doubt, in the sense described at the top of this document — a strong, honestly earned confidence, not a legal finding — and that it would withstand real scrutiny from another careful researcher.

But the verdict is entered here in a standing state of appeal. It is not sealed. If new evidence comes to light — a record surfaces, a DNA match complicates the picture, a document you trusted turns out to be flawed — the case reopens. This isn't a weakness in the verdict. It's the honest acknowledgment that historical research is never actually finished, and treating a conclusion as permanently closed is exactly the kind of overconfidence this whole framework exists to prevent.

What belongs in this stage:

  • Your final determination, stated plainly
  • What specific evidence would be strong enough to reopen the case if it appeared
  • An explicit acknowledgment that the verdict stands only until better evidence says otherwise

Why Build It This Way

Every departure this framework makes from the real courtroom, and from BCG's official standard, was made on purpose, in service of the same underlying goal: catching a researcher's own wishful thinking as early and as thoroughly as possible, and refusing to let a conclusion calcify into unquestionable fact just because it was reached with confidence once.

This is KHGN's own framework — built collaboratively, on our own terms, borrowing what serves the work and leaving behind what doesn't.

This framework was developed by Kentucky History & Genealogy Network, Inc. It is inspired by, but distinct from, both courtroom trial procedure and the Genealogical Proof Standard published by the Board for Certification of Genealogists. It is intended to build sound research habits, not to serve as a substitute for professional certification standards or formal legal procedure.
