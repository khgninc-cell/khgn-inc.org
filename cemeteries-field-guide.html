Cemeteries

A cemetery is a community's record carved in stone. Learn to read the stones and symbols, see what a cemetery reveals about the lives around it, record what you find without harming it, and protect the living along the way.

← Back to Proving Your Claim

To research and understand history, you must place yourself in that time period and work with what was available at that point in history.

Stand in an old cemetery and notice a dozen stones dated the same autumn, or a row of small stones in one family's plot, or a line of graves bearing the same date as a mine explosion. Burials record the forces that shaped people's lives. Read them in their time, and a cemetery tells a story no single record can.

What a Cemetery Reveals

The issues that affected a community, written in stone

EpidemicsClusters of deaths within the same weeks can mark outbreaks, from the cholera epidemics of the 1800s to the 1918 influenza.
WarsGovernment-issued military markers and veterans' emblems record service, from the Revolution through the Civil War and beyond.
DisastersSeveral stones bearing the same date can mark a mine explosion, a flood, or a fire that struck a whole community.
Childhood lossSmall stones, often carved with lambs, record the children families lost young, many of whom appear in no other record.
BelongingFraternal emblems, church sections, segregated burial grounds, and family plots on old farmland show where people belonged.
HardshipUnmarked fieldstones often mean a family couldn't afford a carved marker, not that a burial was unimportant.

A symbol is a lead, not proof. A fraternal emblem points to lodge records, and a military marker points to service records, where the evidence lives. See the Death Records Field Guide for lodge burial records.

Raised Graves, Tent Graves, and Grave Houses

A myth explained by placing yourself in the time

Older Kentucky cemeteries sometimes hold graves covered entirely above ground: raised graves with a capstone spanning the whole grave, tent graves with stone slabs leaning together like a roof, and small grave houses, wooden or stone shelters built over a grave. They're often described as "Indian graves," but in most cases there's a far more practical explanation.

Place yourself in that time. The wills and estate inventories of many early settlers list very little: a few tools, and little iron or steel of any quality. Picture a family burying a loved one with simple hand tools in rocky hillside ground, while cattle and hogs roamed freely among the graves. Two reasons stand out:

  • Graves couldn't always be dug deep. With limited tools and hard, rocky ground, a shallow grave needed protection above it.
  • Livestock grazed among the graves. A heavy cover of stone, slabs, or a small shelter kept graves from being trampled by cattle, or rooted up by hogs and wild animals.

So before calling an unusual grave "Indian," look at the family's estate records, the ground, and the farm around the cemetery. More often than not, the explanation is a family doing the best they could with what they had.

Finding Burials

Where Kentucky's dead may rest

  • Church cemeteries, often with burial registers kept by the church
  • Family cemeteries on farmland, sometimes now on someone else's property, marked by a few stones, a fence line, or a cluster of cedars
  • Community and town cemeteries, and company cemeteries near former coal and timber camps
  • Unmarked graves, shown by sunken ground or plain fieldstones, which may be recorded only in church, funeral home, or family records

Online memorial and grave-listing sites can help you find a burial, but treat their transcriptions as tips. A clear photograph of the stone itself is better evidence.

Recording a Stone: Light and Lens Only

KHGN's method for reading worn inscriptions without harming them

Worn lettering becomes readable when light strikes the stone at a low angle, filling the carved letters with shadow. This is called raking light, and it never touches the stone.

  • A mirror and the sun: reflect sunlight across the face of the stone from the side, at a low angle, and adjust the mirror until the lettering stands out. It works best when the stone itself is in shade.
  • A high-powered battery floodlight: hold it low and to the side, at a sharp angle. It works at any time of day, and especially well at dusk or on cloudy days. Try each side; some letters show better from one direction.
  • Photograph it: take a straight-on shot of the inscription while the light makes it readable, then a wider shot showing the whole stone and its surroundings, and note the cemetery and the stone's location in it.
KHGN never uses:
  • Chalk, flour, or shaving cream, which leave residue that damages stone over time
  • Rubbings, which stress fragile, weathered stones
  • Wire brushes or scraping, which permanently remove the stone's surface
  • Household cleaners or bleach, which cause lasting harm

Respect and Access

Visiting with care

  • Ask permission before visiting a cemetery on private land. Kentucky law provides ways for descendants to visit family cemeteries on private property, but starting with a friendly conversation with the landowner is always best.
  • Leave everything as you found it: flowers, mementos, fallen stones, and fences.
  • Be mindful of families visiting recent graves, and of services in progress.

KHGN documents, photographs, and records cemeteries. KHGN does not perform cemetery maintenance, upkeep, or restoration; the care of cemeteries rests with the counties, local organizations, and individuals who choose to take it on.

Protect the Living

The privacy standards from the Tipple Trial apply here too

Take care with:
  • Companion stones with a living spouse's name and birth year carved beside an open death date, which reveal a likely living person's name, age, and town. Crop or blur that side before sharing, until a death date is confirmed.
  • Recent burials, whose stones and nearby flowers or cards may name living children and grandchildren.
  • Online memorial pages that copy recent obituaries in full, including living survivors' details.

See the Tipple Trial for the full privacy standards.

Quick Practice

Think it through, then reveal the answer

Six stones in one church cemetery are dated within three weeks of each other in the fall of 1918. What might explain it?

Very possibly the 1918 influenza epidemic. Check death records, church registers, and newspapers from those weeks.

An inscription is too worn to read. Should you rub chalk into the letters?

No. Chalk leaves residue that damages stone. Use raking light from a mirror or a battery floodlight held low and to the side, then photograph it.

A companion stone shows "Mary Miller, 1941–" with no death year. Can you post the photo as is?

Crop or blur Mary's side first. The open death date suggests a living person, and the stone reveals her name, age, and town.

An old family cemetery has a grave covered by a stone capstone across its whole length. Is it an Indian burial?

Probably not. Raised graves, tent graves, and grave houses were often built by settler families to protect shallow graves dug with limited tools from livestock and animals. Check the family's estate records and the land before drawing conclusions.

A headstone gives a birth date of 1802, but a Bible record says 1804. Which carries more weight?

Often the Bible entry, if it was written near the time of the birth. Many stones were carved years later from memory. Weigh when and by whom each was made.
