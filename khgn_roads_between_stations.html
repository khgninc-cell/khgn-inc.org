The Roads Between the Stations

How Kentucky's early turnpikes were built, and who built them

A note before you read

This piece is part of the same larger story as KHGN's Road to Kentucky — the roads, forts, and stations that protected early settlers moving through the frontier. But where that story follows one man's path toward land and freedom, this one follows what came after: the roads themselves, and the system that maintained them a generation later.

That system depended, in large part, on enslaved labor.

This is not a comfortable fact, and it isn't offered here as one. It's offered because it's true, because it's part of the documented economic history of early Kentucky, and because an organization dedicated to honest genealogical research doesn't get to look away from the parts of the record that are painful. Many Kentucky families researching their own lineage will eventually find a slaveholding ancestor in the record. This piece exists to help place that discovery in its real historical context — neither excused nor exaggerated, just accurately understood.

From stations to turnpikes

In the earliest years of settlement, the stations described in Road to Kentucky — fortified points spaced roughly a day's ride apart — depended on militia escorts to move people safely between them. As the frontier stabilized through the 1780s and 1790s and the immediate threat of raids diminished, the informal paths connecting those stations gradually became formal roads, and eventually, turnpikes — toll roads maintained by a private operator in exchange for the right to collect fares from travelers.

By the early 1800s, Kentucky's turnpike system had become a significant part of the state's economy. A road's condition determined how quickly crops, goods, and mail could move between towns, which made turnpike contracts valuable, competitive things to hold.

Bidding for a tollgate

A tollgate was typically awarded through a bidding process: a person or company would bid for the right to operate and maintain a specific stretch of road, collecting tolls from travelers in exchange for keeping that stretch passable — grading, clearing, bridging streams, and repairing washouts.

Winning a tollgate contract required real capital. A bidder needed enough standing and resources to be trusted with a public responsibility, and enough labor available to actually maintain miles of frontier road, season after season. This is precisely where the men who had, a generation earlier, arrived in Kentucky as veterans claiming land bounties — men like the composite figure of Conor in KHGN's earlier story — often reappear in the record. Having converted an indenture and a military patent into an established landholding, some of these families had, by the early 1800s, accumulated exactly the kind of capital and standing a tollgate bid required.

And increasingly, by the early 1800s, that capital included enslaved people.

The labor behind the roads

Road maintenance in this period was hard, unglamorous physical labor — clearing timber, breaking stone, digging drainage, repairing washed-out grades by hand, season after season. Tollgate owners who held enslaved people frequently assigned this labor to them, sometimes personally and sometimes by hiring them out to road-maintenance contracts as a source of income in their own right.

This means the very roads that made Kentucky's frontier settlements into a connected, functioning state were, in a direct and documented way, built and maintained by the forced labor of people who held no stake in the freedom, land, or prosperity those roads generated. The same road that might carry a landowner's crops to market, or a family to church, or news of a wedding from one station to the next, was very often kept passable by someone who had no legal right to walk away from that work, no wage of their own, and no say in whether they did it at all.

What the record often leaves out

Most surviving documentation from this period — bid records, court orders, tollgate ledgers — describes this system from the tollgate owner's side: the contract awarded, the toll rates set, the road miles maintained. Enslaved individuals typically appear in these records only indirectly, if at all: as property counted in an estate inventory, as an unnamed line item, or not mentioned at all despite doing the actual work being billed and paid for.

This is itself an important thing for a researcher to sit with. The absence of a person's voice in the historical record is not the absence of that person's experience. Where formerly enslaved people's own accounts do survive — most notably in the WPA Slave Narrative Collection, recorded in the 1930s — they describe, in their own words, exactly this kind of labor: road work, construction, and maintenance performed under compulsion, often for the direct financial benefit of an owner who never touched a shovel. This piece does not attribute invented quotations to specific named individuals; instead, it points researchers directly toward these primary sources, because the people who lived this history are the ones who should be heard telling it, in their own documented words, not paraphrased by anyone else on their behalf.

If your research leads here, the WPA Slave Narrative Collection (held by the Library of Congress) is a genuine and important starting point for hearing this part of Kentucky's history directly from people who lived it.

Sitting honestly with this

For a genealogy organization, this history isn't a detour from the mission — it is the mission, applied without flinching. The same Genealogical Proof Standard covered elsewhere in KHGN's materials — search thoroughly, cite honestly, address what doesn't fit the story you'd prefer — applies here with particular weight. A family history that proudly documents a veteran's land patent while quietly omitting what that same family's prosperity depended on a generation later is not a complete history. It's a selectively edited one.

This doesn't mean every descendant carries personal guilt for what an ancestor did under a system that has since been rightly abolished. It means the honest response to finding this in your own record is not defensiveness, and it isn't erasure — it's acknowledgment. Naming what happened, as plainly and accurately as the record allows, is the only way this part of Kentucky's history gets treated with the seriousness it deserves.

Closing

This was who many Kentucky families were, in a time that permitted — and depended on — this system. It should never be mistaken for who we are now, and it should never again be who anyone is. Remembering it honestly is not a betrayal of family heritage. It's the only version of that heritage worth passing on.
