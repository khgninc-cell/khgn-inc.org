Getting Started in Genealogy

Every family history starts as a mystery. In six short levels, you'll learn to investigate it like a detective: start with your own family, follow your leads to original records, and build a case strong enough to take to court. Each level gives you exactly what the next one needs.

Before you begin

How this training works

Genealogy research has two phases, just like solving a crime. First comes the investigation: gathering leads, checking tips, finding physical evidence, and building a case. That's what this training teaches. Then comes the courtroom: proving your case, which you'll learn next in the KHGN Evidence Framework.

Each level matches one of KHGN's star designations, from ★1 Lead to ★5 Case Built. All six levels are open, but they're built to be taken in order.

Your progress is saved on this device only, so you can stop and pick up where you left off.

Level 1 of 6

Start With Your Family

★1 Lead

Every investigation begins by talking to the people closest to the case. In genealogy, that's your family.

Talk with your relatives

  • Start with the oldest generation you can reach. Their memories reach back furthest, and they won't always be here to ask.
  • Ask open questions: Where did your grandparents live? What did they do for a living? What stories did they tell?
  • Write down who said it and when. "Aunt Ruth, March 2026: Grandpa came from Virginia" is a lead you can follow. "Someone said he came from Virginia" isn't.

Search the house

Family Bibles, letters, funeral cards, old photographs with names on the back, discharge papers, and newspaper clippings are all worth gathering. Note where each one came from and who has it now.

Every story is a lead

A family story is a lead: something worth following, not something proven. Stories often hold a kernel of truth, with details that shifted over generations. Treat each one as a question to investigate: "That's a good lead. Now let's find the record that proves it."

Protect the living

Your living relatives' names, addresses, birth dates, and stories are theirs. Keep them private, and never post them publicly. Your research focuses on ancestors who have passed.

Level 2 of 6

Checking the Tips

★2 Tip

With your leads in hand, you'll find plenty of tips online. A good detective checks every tip before trusting it.

What counts as a tip

  • Online family trees built by other researchers
  • Indexes and databases that list names and dates from records
  • Transcriptions and abstracts, where someone typed up or summarized a record
  • Published family histories and compiled genealogies

Each is secondhand: someone else's reading of the evidence. Tips are valuable because they tell you where to look.

How errors spread

One researcher guesses, another copies the guess, and soon dozens of trees "agree." But twenty copies of the same mistake are still one mistake. This is the most common trap in genealogy, and why KHGN never treats a copied tree as proof.

Use tips to find the original

When a tip says "born 1823, Madison County," ask: what record says so? Follow it back to the original document. If no one can name the source, it's still only a tip.

Level 3 of 6

Finding Physical Evidence

★3 Physical Evidence

Following your leads and tips back to the original record is where the case gets real.

What physical evidence looks like

  • Census records: a household, as the census taker wrote it down
  • Marriage bonds and licenses: in early Kentucky, a groom often posted a bond with a bondsman, frequently a relative, before marrying
  • Deeds and wills: land and property, often naming family relationships
  • Vital records: birth and death certificates, where they exist
  • Court, tax, and church records

Where Kentucky's records are

County clerk offices hold deeds, wills, and marriage records. The Kentucky Department for Libraries and Archives, free genealogy websites like FamilySearch, and the Library of Congress's digitized newspapers hold many more. KYGenWeb's county pages are a great place to start. For a decade-by-decade look at what each census asked, see the Census Field Guide.

Cite it so anyone can find it

Every piece of physical evidence needs a citation that answers: who created it, what it is, when, where it's kept, and where in it the information appears. For example: 1850 U.S. Census, Rockcastle County, Kentucky, page 12, household of William Smith. A source no one else can find can't be verified.

Level 4 of 6

Corroboration

★4 Corroborated

One record can be wrong. When independent records agree, your case gets much stronger.

Weighing witness statements

Every record has a witness: the person who gave the information. A mother reporting her baby's birth was there. A son reporting his father's birth date on a death certificate decades later wasn't. Ask of every record: who gave this information, and how would they know?

Independent means different origins

  • Independent records were created separately, by different people for different reasons, like a census, a deed, and a will.
  • Copies aren't corroboration. Three websites showing the same census record are one record.
  • Redundant records add nothing new. Five census years showing the same man in the same county don't prove who his parents were.

Level 5 of 6

Building the Case

★5 Case Built

Some answers are never written down in one place. Detectives solve those cases with circumstantial evidence.

Circumstantial evidence

No record may say "John was Elijah's father." But if John's land sits next to Elijah's, John witnesses Elijah's deed, Elijah names a son John, and both move to the same new county together, those facts point to a relationship. Genealogists call this indirect evidence. Assembled carefully, it can solve cases no single record can.

Follow the friends, associates, and neighbors

People lived, worked, married, and migrated alongside their communities. The neighbors, witnesses, bondsmen, and fellow church members around your ancestor often lead to the family you're looking for.

Check the alibis

When two men share a name, look for alibis: records placing each one somewhere else. If "your" John Smith is in Rockcastle County on the same day another John Smith signs a deed in Laurel County, they're two different men. Merging them is one of the most common errors in genealogy.

Resolve conflicts, and keep a case file

When records disagree, don't pick your favorite. Weigh who gave each piece of information and how they'd know, and explain your reasoning. Keep everything in a case file: your leads, tips, physical evidence, citations, and notes on how it fits together.

Level 6 of 6

The Big Picture

Here's how everything you've learned fits together, and where the climb goes next.

You've just climbed the investigation: gathering leads from your family, checking tips, finding physical evidence, corroborating it, and building a case. That's the work behind KHGN's first five star designations.

The courtroom is next. When your case is built, you prove it using the KHGN Evidence Framework: Opening Statements, Testimony and Rebuttal, Closing Arguments, and a Verdict that's never fully certain. Practice on Same Name, Wrong Man, then build your own with Build Your Case. A fully proven case, verified by two reviewers, earns the highest designation: the Gold Star Verdict.

Want to see where your own research stands? Try Rate Your Research, a free self-evaluation using these designations. And before you lean on AI tools, take Working With AI Responsibly.

Stars come from proving more, not from attaching more.
